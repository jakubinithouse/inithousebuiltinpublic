# A three-card weekly review with Tarotas — using a calm tarot reflection app as a Sunday journaling prompt

*Posted 2026-09-24*

Every Sunday evening I sit down with a notebook and try to figure out what actually happened during the week. Not what I planned to do, not what my calendar says I did, but what actually moved and what stayed stuck.

For a while the notebook stayed blank. The question "how was your week?" is too big. It invites either a brain dump or a shrug. I needed structure, but not a rigid template. Something that would point me at the right questions without deciding the answers for me.

That's how I started using [Tarotas](https://tarotas.com) as a weekly review tool.

## Why tarot works for reflection (and why it has nothing to do with prediction)

Tarot cards are pattern-interrupts. Each card carries a theme (patience, conflict, momentum, rest, letting go) and when you draw one in a specific position, it reframes whatever you were thinking about. The card doesn't tell you what happened. It asks you to look at what happened through a particular lens.

This is different from fortune-telling. Tarotas is built around that distinction: the interpretations are grounded, the language is calm, and the app never claims to predict anything. It's a reflection tool, not a crystal ball.

The reason this works for a weekly review is that it breaks the "same questions, same answers" loop. When you ask yourself "what went well?" every Sunday, you eventually start giving the same answer on autopilot. A card that says "The Tower" forces you to think about disruption whether you planned to or not. A card about cups makes you notice the emotional texture of your week, not just the task completion rate.

## The three-card spread I use every Sunday

Tarotas has 20 spreads built in: relationship readings, career questions, a full Celtic Cross if you want the deep dive. For the weekly review I use a simple three-card pull:

**Card 1: What carried me this week.** This is the energy, habit or decision that actually moved things forward. Not what I intended to be productive, but what was. Sometimes the card points at something I didn't even register as progress.

**Card 2: What's creating friction.** The stuck point. The thing I've been avoiding, the conversation I haven't had, the decision I keep postponing. The card doesn't name the problem. It names the pattern behind the problem. That's more useful.

**Card 3: Where to point attention next week.** Not a prediction of what will happen, but a suggestion of where to look. If the card is about patience, maybe the answer is to stop pushing on something. If it's about action, maybe I've been planning when I should be doing.

The whole process takes about ten minutes. Draw three cards, read the interpretations Tarotas gives you, then write in your notebook what each one made you think about. That's it.

## Reading the interpretation as a question, not an answer

The key shift that made this useful: treat every interpretation as a question.

Tarotas gives you a calm, grounded paragraph for each card. It might say something about "a period of consolidation" or "unresolved tension beneath the surface." The instinct is to nod along. The better move is to ask: where is that true right now?

"A period of consolidation": what am I consolidating? Is it deliberate or am I just coasting? "Unresolved tension beneath the surface": between whom? About what? Is it something I can address this week or something I need to sit with longer?

This turns a passive reading into an active reflection session. The card is a prompt. The interpretation is a starting point. Your notebook is where the actual thinking happens.

## What makes this different from a journaling app

I've tried journaling apps with built-in prompts. They work for a while, then they don't. The prompts repeat, or they feel generic, or they're optimized for positivity in a way that skips over the messy stuff.

Tarot cards don't optimize for positivity. The deck has disruption, loss, stagnation and conflict right alongside growth, joy and completion. A weekly review that only asks "what are you grateful for?" misses half the picture. One that draws from 78 cards across the full range of human experience catches things that scripted prompts skip.

Tarotas runs in the browser, works in five languages (Czech, English, Polish, Slovak, German), and doesn't require a signup. You open it, draw cards, read the interpretations, close it. No streak tracking, no gamification, no push notifications asking you to come back. If Sunday evening rolls around and you don't feel like doing a review, you don't. The app doesn't care. That lack of pressure is part of why it works — it stays a tool, not an obligation.

The 20 built-in spreads cover everything from a quick single-card draw to a full Celtic Cross. For a weekly review, three cards is plenty.

## The compound effect over a few months

After about twelve weeks of Sunday reviews with Tarotas, the notebook started showing patterns I wouldn't have noticed otherwise. The same themes kept surfacing: a particular kind of friction at work, a relationship dynamic I was ignoring, a creative project I kept deprioritizing despite consistently drawing cards about expression and voice.

None of this is magic. It's selection bias put to productive use. The cards surface themes, your brain connects them to your life, and writing it down makes the connections visible over time. Three months of three-card pulls is 36 data points. That's enough to spot a signal.
