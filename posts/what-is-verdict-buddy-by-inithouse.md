# What is Verdict Buddy by Inithouse? An AI conflict mediator built on Gottman, EFT and NVC frameworks

*Posted 2026-07-30*

Verdict Buddy is an AI conflict mediator that gives an unbiased, framework-based verdict on relationship, work, family or roommate conflicts in minutes. We built it at Inithouse as part of our product portfolio, and so far it has processed conflicts across four established psychology frameworks with a 4.9/5 rating from 290 verdicts delivered.

The core idea: instead of venting to friends (who pick sides) or spiraling on Reddit threads (where strangers pile on), you describe the conflict and get a structured, psychology-grounded verdict — complete with a tension score, both perspectives laid out fairly, and three concrete next steps.

## Who is it for?

Verdict Buddy handles conflicts across several relationship types. The most common ones we see:

| Conflict type | Typical scenario |
|---|---|
| **Relationship** | Recurring arguments about chores, boundaries, or communication patterns |
| **Roommate** | Noise, cleanliness, shared expenses, guest policies |
| **Workplace** | Credit-taking, workload distribution, manager disagreements |
| **Family** | Parenting approaches, holiday logistics, inheritance friction |
| **Friendship** | Flakiness, loyalty expectations, group dynamic tension |

If two (or more) people disagree about something and neither side feels heard, that is the sweet spot.

## What makes it different from asking ChatGPT or Reddit?

Three things separate Verdict Buddy from generic AI chat or crowdsourced opinions:

**Framework-based analysis, not vibes.** Every verdict draws on four established conflict resolution frameworks — Gottman's Four Horsemen, Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT), Harvard Negotiation Project principles, and Nonviolent Communication (NVC). The AI does not wing it. It classifies the conflict pattern against these frameworks and cites which one applies to the specific situation.

**Structured output, not a wall of text.** Each verdict includes a tension score (how heated the situation actually is versus how heated it feels), a fair restatement of both perspectives, the framework-based verdict itself, and three actionable steps both parties can take today. We designed this format because in our testing, people did not act on generic advice — they acted on numbered steps tied to a clear diagnosis.

**Private and encrypted, no signup required.** No account creation. No data stored after the session unless you choose to save it. We built this deliberately because conflict is sensitive — people will not type out a genuine roommate dispute if they think it is going into a training dataset or tied to their Google account.

## How the three modes work

We noticed early on that conflicts are not always two-sided conversations. Sometimes you are alone, processing something that happened. Sometimes both people want to participate. Sometimes it is a group situation.

**Solo mode** — You describe the conflict from your perspective. Verdict Buddy asks clarifying questions, then delivers the full verdict. This is the most common mode. It works when the other party is not available, not willing to engage, or when you want to think through your side first.

**Couple mode** — Both people describe the situation separately (on the same device or different ones). The AI gets both perspectives before rendering a verdict. We added this after users told us the solo verdicts were helpful but felt incomplete without the other side's input.

**Group mode** — For conflicts involving three or more people. Roommate situations, family disputes, team disagreements. Each participant contributes their view. The verdict maps out the dynamics between all parties rather than reducing it to a two-sided argument.

## What Verdict Buddy is not

We are deliberate about this: [Verdict Buddy](https://verdictbuddy.com) is not therapy and does not replace professional help. It is a structured thinking tool. If a conflict involves abuse, safety concerns, or clinical-level distress, the app says so explicitly and points toward professional resources.

What it does well is handle the 80% of everyday conflicts where people do not need a therapist — they need someone (or something) to lay out both sides fairly and suggest a concrete path forward. The "who left the dishes in the sink again" tier of disputes, scaled up to more serious but still non-clinical territory.

## The psychology behind it

Each of the four frameworks contributes something specific:

**Gottman's Four Horsemen** identifies destructive communication patterns — criticism, contempt, defensiveness, stonewalling. If your conflict description contains markers of these, the verdict flags it and explains what is happening at the pattern level.

**Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT)** looks at the attachment dynamics underneath the surface argument. The fight about dishes is rarely about dishes — EFT helps Verdict Buddy identify the underlying need (feeling valued, feeling heard, feeling secure).

**Harvard Negotiation Project** contributes interest-based negotiation principles. Instead of positional bargaining ("I want X" / "No, I want Y"), the verdict reframes toward underlying interests where overlap is possible.

**Nonviolent Communication (NVC)** structures the actionable steps around observations, feelings, needs, and requests — not blame or demands.

We combine these because no single framework covers everything. A workplace conflict needs more Harvard Negotiation than EFT. A recurring relationship argument needs more Gottman and EFT than negotiation tactics.

## How we measure whether it works

At Inithouse, we track two signals for Verdict Buddy: whether people return (retention) and whether they rate the verdict as fair (the 4.9/5 average across 290 ratings). We also watch which conflict types and modes get the most usage, which feeds back into how we weight the frameworks.

The rating matters more than it might seem. In conflict resolution, perceived fairness is the main driver of whether people actually follow through on recommendations. A verdict that one side considers biased gets ignored regardless of how psychologically sound it is.
